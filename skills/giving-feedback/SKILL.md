# Giving Feedback

Giving feedback well means delivering observations about someone's
behavior or work in a way that's clear, actionable, and respectful —
distinct from vague praise, unstructured criticism, or feedback that's
really just venting. See [[getting-feedback]] for the receiving side of
the same exchange.

## Core principles

- **Be specific, not general** — "the client section of that report was
  hard to follow because the pricing came before the problem statement"
  is actionable; "this needs work" isn't.
- **Focus on behavior and impact, not character** — describe what someone
  did and its effect, not who they are ("this missed the deadline"
  rather than "you're unreliable"). Behavior can change; character
  judgments just produce defensiveness.
- **Time it close to the event** — feedback given days or weeks later
  loses context and impact; timely feedback (while still being
  thoughtful, not reactive) is more useful and easier to act on.
- **Balance the ledger honestly** — don't manufacture positive feedback
  to soften a critical point (people learn to distrust praise that
  always precedes bad news), but do give genuine positive feedback when
  it's warranted, not only when something's wrong.
- **Make it actionable** — feedback that identifies a problem without
  suggesting a direction forward leaves the recipient stuck; where
  possible, point toward what "better" would look like.

## Delivery matters

Choose a setting appropriate to the feedback's weight — a quick,
positive note can happen in passing; substantive constructive feedback
usually deserves a private, unhurried conversation (see
[[one-on-one-meeting]], [[crucial-conversations]] for handling a
harder version of this). Check in on how the feedback landed rather
than assuming it was received as intended.

## Common pitfalls

- **The "feedback sandwich"** (praise–criticism–praise) used
  mechanically — recipients often learn to recognize the pattern and
  discount the praise on either side as just padding for the criticism.
- **Vague feedback that doesn't specify what to do differently** —
  "communicate better" gives the recipient nothing concrete to change.
- **Delivering feedback in front of others** when it's about an
  individual's development — this often triggers defensiveness rather
  than reflection, regardless of how well-intentioned the content is.
- **Giving feedback only when something's wrong** — if positive feedback
  never happens, critical feedback starts to feel like the only kind
  that exists, which discourages people from wanting to hear it at all.
