# Communications Manager

The communications management function owns how an organization
communicates with its **internal** audiences — employees, leadership
messaging, change announcements — as distinct from
[[public-relations-manager]] (external, media/reputation-facing) and
[[marketing-manager]] (external, demand-generation-facing). See
[[ways-of-working]] for team-level day-to-day communication norms
this function operates at a larger, more structured scale above.

## Audience segmentation

Not every message suits every audience the same way — a change affecting
one department needs different depth and framing than an
all-company update, and senior leaders often need earlier, more detailed
briefing than a company-wide announcement to avoid being blindsided by
their own teams asking questions first. Planning **who hears what, in what
order** is as much the job as writing the message itself.

## Change communications

Communicating organizational change (restructuring, a new strategy, a
merger, a leadership change) has a well-known shape worth planning
deliberately: explain **why** the change is happening before **what** is
changing, acknowledge the disruption honestly rather than only the
positive framing, give people a channel to ask questions and get real
answers (not just a one-way broadcast), and follow up — a single
announcement without follow-through reads as the organization having
moved on while the people affected haven't.

## Leadership messaging

Communications often ghostwrites or coaches leadership communication
(all-hands scripts, town-hall Q&A prep, a CEO's company-wide email) —
the job includes translating what a leader wants to convey into language
that lands with the actual audience, and preparing leaders for the
questions a message will predictably provoke rather than leaving them to
improvise under pressure.

## Channel strategy

Matching the message to the right channel: a nuanced, sensitive
announcement usually needs a synchronous, human channel (a town hall, a
manager-led team conversation) rather than a cold email, while routine
operational updates suit an asynchronous channel — see
[[ways-of-working]]'s asynchronous/communications guidance for the
underlying principles this function applies at organizational scale.
Over-relying on one channel (email for everything, regardless of
sensitivity) is a common, avoidable failure mode.

## Measuring effectiveness

Unlike a marketing funnel, internal communications effectiveness is
harder to quantify with a single clean metric — common proxies include
survey-based understanding/sentiment checks after a major announcement,
engagement with internal channels, and (most usefully but hardest to get)
direct qualitative feedback on whether people actually understood and
believed the message, not just whether they received it.

## Common pitfalls

- **Leading with "what" before "why"** in a change announcement — without
  the reasoning, the change reads as arbitrary even when it isn't, and
  rumor fills the gap the "why" should have occupied.
- **One-way broadcast with no channel for questions** — leaves confusion
  and misinformation to circulate informally instead of being addressed
  directly.
- **Leadership blindsided by their own team's questions** — a failure of
  sequencing: senior stakeholders should generally hear significant news
  before their direct reports do, not simultaneously or after.
- **Treating a single announcement as "communicated"** — sustained
  understanding usually needs repetition and follow-up, not one message
  assumed to have landed.
- **Uniform channel choice regardless of message sensitivity** — a
  significant, emotionally loaded change delivered only via email reads
  as impersonal regardless of how carefully the text was written.
