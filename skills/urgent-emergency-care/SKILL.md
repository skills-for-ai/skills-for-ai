# Urgent & Emergency Care

**If a real emergency is actually happening, the answer is always: call
the local emergency number now (999/112/911, or the local equivalent).**
This skill is general educational information about how emergency and
urgent care systems are organized and what counts as which — it is not a
substitute for that call, and it cannot triage a real, specific situation.
See [[first-aid]] for hands-on technique while waiting for help.

## Emergency vs. urgent vs. routine care

Most health systems distinguish three tiers, and picking the wrong one
either delays life-saving care or overwhelms emergency services with
non-emergencies:

- **Emergency** — an immediate threat to life, limb, or long-term health.
  Call emergency services or go to an emergency department (ED/A&E)
  immediately. Examples widely used in public guidance: chest pain,
  difficulty breathing, signs of stroke, severe uncontrolled bleeding,
  loss of consciousness, severe allergic reaction (anaphylaxis), suspected
  major fracture or spinal injury, severe burns.
- **Urgent** — needs same-day or next-day attention but isn't immediately
  life-threatening: a high fever that won't come down, a minor fracture, a
  wound that needs stitches but isn't bleeding severely, a bad ear or
  urinary tract infection. This is what urgent care clinics / walk-in
  centers / same-day GP appointments exist for — using an ED for this tier
  means waiting behind genuine emergencies and adding load a less
  time-critical service could absorb instead.
- **Routine** — can wait for a scheduled primary care appointment: ongoing
  management of a known condition, non-urgent symptoms, prescription
  renewals, preventive care.

## Recognizing common emergencies (widely-taught public signs)

- **Stroke — FAST**: **F**ace drooping on one side, **A**rm weakness on one
  side, **S**peech difficulty or slurring, **T**ime to call emergency
  services immediately. Time-to-treatment directly affects outcome, which
  is why this specific mnemonic is taught publicly rather than a longer
  clinical checklist.
- **Heart attack**: chest pain/pressure/tightness (may radiate to arm, jaw,
  or back), shortness of breath, sweating, nausea — note that symptoms can
  present differently and less classically in women.
- **Anaphylaxis (severe allergic reaction)**: swelling of face/throat,
  difficulty breathing, widespread hives, dizziness or collapse — call
  emergency services and use an epinephrine auto-injector immediately if
  the person has one prescribed; this is not a "wait and see" situation.
- **Severe bleeding, major trauma, suspected spinal injury, loss of
  consciousness, seizure lasting more than a few minutes** — all warrant
  an immediate emergency call.

## What to say when calling emergency services

Dispatchers are trained to extract this efficiently, but knowing the shape
helps: **location** first (the single most critical piece — give it even
before finishing describing the problem, in case the call drops), **what
happened**, **how many people are affected**, the **casualty's condition**
(conscious/breathing?), and **your name and callback number**. Stay on the
line — dispatchers often give live first-aid instructions while help is
on the way, and hanging up early loses that guidance.

## Common pitfalls

- **Under-reacting to "atypical" emergency presentations** — e.g. assuming
  chest discomfort that doesn't match a textbook description isn't serious,
  or that a stroke can't be happening because the person is young. Public
  guidance mnemonics (FAST, etc.) exist precisely because atypical
  presentations are common and easy to dismiss.
- **Driving yourself or someone else to the ED during a suspected heart
  attack or stroke** instead of calling emergency services — ambulance
  crews can begin treatment en route and alert the receiving hospital,
  which a private vehicle can't do.
- **Using an ED for an urgent-but-not-emergency issue** out of uncertainty
  about where else to go — most regions have a non-emergency medical
  helpline (in the UK, NHS 111; other regions have equivalents) specifically
  to help make that call.
- **Waiting to "see if it gets better" on classic emergency signs**
  (crushing chest pain, one-sided weakness, severe breathing difficulty) —
  these are exactly the presentations where the guidance is to act
  immediately, not to observe further.
- **Treating this skill, or any general write-up, as sufficient to rule an
  emergency out** — the guidance here is for recognizing when to seek real
  help fast, never for concluding a real, present situation is safe to
  ignore.
