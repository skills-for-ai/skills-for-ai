# Marketing Manager

Marketing management owns demand generation and brand positioning — making
the right audience aware of and interested in an offering — distinct from
[[sales-manager]] (converting interested prospects into paying
customers) and [[public-relations-manager]] (managing an
organization's reputation and third-party narrative, which isn't the same
as promoting a product directly).

## Positioning and the marketing mix

- **Positioning** — the specific place a brand/product occupies in a
  target customer's mind relative to alternatives; a positioning
  statement typically names the target audience, the category, the key
  benefit, and the reason to believe it over alternatives.
- **The marketing mix ("4 Ps")** — Product, Price, Place (distribution),
  Promotion — the classic framework for the levers marketing coordinates
  across (later extended with People, Process, Physical evidence for
  services). Useful as a checklist that campaign planning isn't only
  about the promotion lever while ignoring pricing/distribution fit.

## The funnel and metrics

```
Awareness → Interest → Consideration → Conversion → Retention/Advocacy
```

- **CAC (Customer Acquisition Cost)** — total marketing (and often sales)
  spend divided by new customers acquired in the period.
- **LTV (Customer Lifetime Value)** — the total value a customer generates
  over the relationship, not just at first purchase.
- **LTV:CAC ratio** — a widely-used health check; a ratio too close to 1:1
  means acquisition spend barely pays for itself, while a very high ratio
  can actually indicate under-investment in growth, not just efficiency.
- **Conversion rate** at each funnel stage — where prospects are being
  lost matters more than the aggregate conversion number, since different
  stages need different fixes (awareness problem vs. a checkout-flow
  problem are not solved the same way).

## Campaign planning

A campaign needs a clear **objective** (awareness, lead generation,
retention, …), a defined **target audience/segment**, a **message**
grounded in the positioning above, chosen **channels** matched to where
that audience actually is, and **success metrics** decided before launch —
not chosen afterward to justify whatever happened to move.

## Segmentation and channels

- **Segmentation** — dividing a market into groups with meaningfully
  different needs or behavior (demographic, behavioral, needs-based), so
  messaging and channel choice can be tailored rather than uniformly
  broadcast.
- **Owned / earned / paid media** — owned (a company's own website, email
  list), earned (organic press, word of mouth — closely tied to
  [[public-relations-manager]]'s work), and paid (advertising) —
  each with different cost, credibility, and control tradeoffs; a
  marketing plan relying on only one of the three is fragile to that
  single channel's cost or algorithm changes.

## Common pitfalls

- **Optimizing top-of-funnel metrics (impressions, clicks) that don't
  connect to revenue** — vanity metrics that move without affecting
  conversion or retention give false confidence.
- **A positioning statement crafted internally without checking it against
  how customers actually describe the problem** — an elegant internal
  narrative that doesn't match the target audience's own language usually
  underperforms a blunter one that does.
- **Running a campaign with no pre-defined success metric** — "see how it
  goes" invites post-hoc rationalization of whatever number looks best
  afterward.
- **Confusing marketing's job with sales' job** — marketing generates and
  qualifies demand; expecting a marketing campaign alone to close deals
  (or blaming marketing for a sales-execution problem) misattributes where
  in the funnel a real issue sits.
