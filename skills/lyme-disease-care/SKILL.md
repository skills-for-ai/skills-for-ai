# Lyme Disease Care

> ⚠️ **Ask your doctor or health care professional. This information is AI generated for education and NOT medical advice.**

Lyme disease is a bacterial infection transmitted by the bite of an
infected tick. Early treatment with antibiotics is generally effective;
untreated infection can spread and cause more serious complications.

## Common symptoms

A characteristic expanding "bullseye" rash (in many, but not all,
cases) at the bite site, fever, fatigue, headache, muscle and joint
aches — and, if untreated, potential later symptoms affecting joints,
the nervous system, or heart.

## General self-care measures

- Complete the full prescribed course of antibiotics exactly as
  directed if diagnosed.
- Remove any attached tick promptly and correctly (see
  [[tick-bite-care]]) to reduce transmission risk.
- Monitor the bite site and overall symptoms for several weeks after a
  tick bite, since a rash can appear days to weeks later.
- Take precautions to prevent future tick bites in tick-prone areas
  (protective clothing, tick checks, repellent).

## When to seek medical attention

- A tick bite followed by a rash, fever, or flu-like symptoms.
- Any expanding rash after a tick bite, even without other symptoms.
- Symptoms affecting joints, nerves, or heart rhythm developing weeks
  after a tick bite, which can indicate later-stage disease.
- A tick bite in an area where Lyme disease is known to be common,
  even without immediate symptoms — discuss with a doctor about
  monitoring or preventive treatment.

## Common pitfalls

- **Waiting for the classic bullseye rash before seeking care** — not
  everyone with Lyme disease develops this rash; other symptoms after a
  tick bite still warrant evaluation.
- **Removing a tick incorrectly** (crushing it, using heat or chemicals)
  — this can increase transmission risk; proper removal technique
  matters (see [[tick-bite-care]]).
- **Assuming symptoms resolve on their own without treatment** —
  untreated Lyme disease can progress to more serious, harder-to-treat
  complications.
