# Finance Manager

Finance management owns budgeting, financial reporting, and the financial
lens on business decisions — distinct from personal finance or investment
advice, which this skill does not provide.

## The three core financial statements

- **Income statement (P&L)** — revenue minus expenses over a period,
  ending in net profit/loss; answers "did we make money in this period."
- **Balance sheet** — assets, liabilities, and equity at a single point in
  time; answers "what do we own and owe right now," and must always
  balance (assets = liabilities + equity).
- **Cash flow statement** — actual cash moving in and out, split into
  operating, investing, and financing activities; answers "do we have
  enough cash," which a profitable P&L doesn't guarantee (a business can
  be profitable on paper and still run out of cash if revenue is booked
  before it's collected).

A team unfamiliar with all three often conflates profitability with cash
health — a genuinely dangerous gap, since running out of cash can end a
business a P&L alone wouldn't have predicted.

## Budgeting and forecasting

- **Budget** — a planned allocation of spend/revenue for a period, set in
  advance.
- **Forecast** — an updated, ongoing estimate of what will actually
  happen, revised as new information arrives — a forecast that's never
  updated after the initial budget is just a stale budget wearing a
  different label.
- **Variance analysis** — comparing actual results to budget/forecast and
  explaining *why* the gap exists, not just reporting that it exists —
  the explanation is what makes variance analysis useful for the next
  decision rather than only a historical record.

## Unit economics

- **Gross margin** — revenue minus direct cost of delivering the
  product/service, as a percentage of revenue; the ceiling on how much is
  left to cover everything else (overhead, growth investment, profit).
- **Contribution margin** — similar concept at the level of one unit/
  customer/transaction, useful for deciding whether more volume actually
  helps or just scales a loss.
- **Break-even point** — the volume at which total revenue equals total
  costs; useful for sanity-checking whether a growth plan is heading
  toward sustainability or just toward a bigger loss at scale.

## Business case financial justification

A well-formed financial case for an investment states: the **expected
return** (revenue, cost savings, risk avoidance — quantified where
possible), the **cost** (all-in, not just the most visible line item), the
**payback period** or ROI, and the **key assumptions and their
sensitivity** — a business case with no stated assumptions can't be
usefully challenged or later checked against what actually happened; one
that names its assumptions can be, which is a feature, not a weakness.

## Common pitfalls

- **Confusing profit with cash** — see above; a growing, profitable
  business can still fail from a cash-flow gap (e.g. slow customer
  payment terms funding fast-growing costs).
- **A budget treated as fixed** regardless of how conditions change during
  the period, rather than revised via ongoing forecasting — leads to
  decisions made against stale numbers.
- **A business case with hidden or unstated assumptions** — makes the
  case unfalsifiable in the moment and impossible to learn from later when
  actuals diverge from the projection.
- **Ignoring all-in cost** — evaluating an investment against only its
  most visible cost line (e.g. a software license) while ignoring
  implementation, training, and ongoing support cost understates the real
  total.
- **Treating this or any general description as investment/tax/personal
  financial advice** — it describes an organizational function; a real
  financial or investment decision needs a qualified professional.
