# Hay Fever Care

> ⚠️ **Ask your doctor or health care professional. This information is AI generated for education and NOT medical advice.**

Hay fever (allergic rhinitis) is an allergic reaction to airborne
substances like pollen, causing cold-like symptoms, typically seasonal
but sometimes year-round (perennial allergic rhinitis).

## Common symptoms

Sneezing, runny or blocked nose, itchy or watery eyes, itchy throat or
ears, and fatigue, often worse at certain times of year or in specific
environments.

## General self-care measures

- Identify and reduce exposure to triggers (keep windows closed during
  high pollen periods, shower after being outdoors, check pollen
  forecasts).
- Take antihistamines or other prescribed/recommended medication
  proactively during known trigger seasons, not just after symptoms
  start.
- Consider a nasal saline rinse for symptom relief.
- Discuss allergy testing or immunotherapy for persistent, significant
  symptoms.

## When to seek medical attention

- Symptoms significantly affecting sleep, work, or daily life despite
  over-the-counter treatment.
- Symptoms that don't fit the typical hay fever pattern (year-round
  without an obvious environmental trigger, or with additional symptoms
  like facial pain).
- Wheezing or shortness of breath alongside hay fever symptoms — may
  indicate co-occurring asthma needing its own management.

## Common pitfalls

- **Starting antihistamines only after symptoms are already severe** —
  starting medication before or at the very start of a known trigger
  season tends to control symptoms better than reacting after they
  peak.
- **Assuming all nasal/eye symptoms are hay fever** — infections and
  other conditions (like sinusitis) can present similarly; a pattern
  that doesn't fit typical seasonal allergy timing is worth checking.
- **Ignoring asthma symptoms that coexist with hay fever** — allergic
  rhinitis and asthma often occur together, and unmanaged upper-airway
  allergy symptoms can worsen asthma control.
