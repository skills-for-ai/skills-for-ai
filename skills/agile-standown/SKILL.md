# Agile Stand-Down

A "stand-down" is an informal team practice some organizations use as a
closing counterpart to the morning [[agile-standup]] — a brief
end-of-day (or end-of-session) check-in summarizing what was actually
accomplished, rather than what's planned. **Unlike the standup, this
isn't a standardized term with one universally agreed format** — it
shows up under this name in some distributed/remote teams and in
organizations with a military or emergency-services background where
"stand-down" already means "end of an active period," but a given team
using the term may mean something a little different. Confirm what a
specific team actually intends before assuming a fixed structure.

## A common shape, where it's used

Where teams do run one, it typically mirrors the standup's brevity and
timebox but flips the temporal focus:

- What did I actually get done today (as opposed to what was planned this
  morning)?
- Is there anything unfinished that needs to be flagged before the day
  ends, so it's not silently carried over unnoticed?
- Any blocker that needs attention before tomorrow, rather than waiting
  until the next morning's standup to surface it?

## Why a team might want one

A morning standup plans the day; a stand-down closes it out — for teams
spanning time zones, an end-of-day stand-down from one group can hand
off open items to a team starting their day elsewhere, which a
morning-only standup structure doesn't naturally support. It can also
catch a blocker before it sits unaddressed overnight, rather than only
surfacing it at the next morning's standup.

## Relationship to standup and reflection

A stand-down isn't the same as [[agile-reflection]]'s retrospective
practice — a stand-down is a brief, daily, factual check-out; a
retrospective is a deeper, less frequent (commonly per-sprint)
examination of process and behavior change. Nor is it a requirement of
any major agile framework the way the standup effectively is in Scrum —
treat it as an optional practice a team can adopt if it solves a real
coordination problem (like time-zone handoff), not as something every
agile team is expected to run.

## Common pitfalls

- **Assuming "stand-down" means the same thing across every team** —
  given the lack of a standardized definition, confirm what a specific
  team means before facilitating or documenting one on their behalf.
- **Running both a standup and a stand-down with identical content** —
  if the stand-down just repeats the same three standup questions
  without the forward/backward temporal distinction, it's redundant
  meeting overhead rather than a genuinely useful addition.
- **Treating it as a substitute for a real retrospective** — a daily
  factual check-out doesn't provide the deeper process reflection
  [[agile-reflection]] is meant to produce.
