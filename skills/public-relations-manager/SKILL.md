# Public Relations Manager

Public relations manages an organization's reputation and its narrative in
front of external audiences — media, the public, regulators, industry
influencers — through **earned** attention (press coverage, third-party
commentary) rather than paid placement, distinct from
[[marketing-manager]]'s direct demand-generation work and
[[communications-manager]]'s internal-audience focus.

## Media relations

- **Press release** — a written announcement (product launch, funding,
  leadership change, major milestone) formatted for journalists to use or
  quote from directly; leads with the most newsworthy fact, not a
  marketing headline.
- **Media pitch** — a direct, individually-tailored outreach to a specific
  journalist/outlet explaining why a story matters to *their* audience
  specifically — a generic mass-blasted pitch is far less effective than
  one referencing the journalist's own recent coverage and beat.
- **Building relationships with journalists** before there's a specific ask
  — PR that only shows up when a favor is needed has far less credibility
  than PR that's a known, reliable source of relevant information over
  time.

## Crisis and reputation management

- Speed and honesty matter more than a polished statement delayed for
  perfection — a slow or evasive initial response is frequently what
  turns an incident into a reputational crisis, more than the incident
  itself.
- A basic crisis-communication structure: **acknowledge** the situation
  quickly, state **what's being done** about it, commit to a **follow-up**
  with more detail once it's actually known — rather than either silence
  or over-promising specifics before they're confirmed.
- **Message discipline** — in a crisis, a small number of designated
  spokespeople with an agreed, consistent set of facts prevents
  contradictory statements from different parts of the organization, which
  independently damages credibility beyond the original issue.

## Stakeholder communications strategy

PR's audience is broader than "the press" — regulators, industry analysts,
investors, and community groups are all stakeholders whose perception
matters and who often need different messaging emphasis on the same
underlying facts, coordinated so the different messages don't contradict
each other if compared side by side.

## Measuring PR

Historically hard to quantify cleanly; common (imperfect) measures include
share of voice (coverage volume/sentiment relative to competitors), message
pull-through (how much of the intended message actually appears in
resulting coverage), and sentiment analysis — all directional indicators,
none a precise causal measure of business impact the way a marketing
funnel conversion rate can be.

## Common pitfalls

- **Going silent during a crisis** waiting for complete information —
  silence is read as evasion far more often than as prudence; an honest
  "here's what we know so far and what we're doing to find out more" beats
  no statement at all.
- **Over-promising specifics before they're confirmed** in an early crisis
  statement — a walked-back claim compounds the reputational damage of the
  original issue.
- **Treating PR as free advertising** — earned media works because it's
  independent; pushing a message so promotional that a journalist won't
  run it as-is (or runs it skeptically) undermines the credibility
  advantage earned coverage has over paid placement.
- **No pre-agreed spokesperson/messaging discipline before a crisis hits**
  — figuring this out in the moment, under pressure, is how contradictory
  statements happen.
