# Tick Bite Care

> ⚠️ **Ask your doctor or health care professional. This information is AI generated for education and NOT medical advice.**

Ticks are small parasites that can transmit infections (including Lyme
disease) through their bite. Prompt, correct removal reduces
transmission risk.

## Proper tick removal

- Use fine-tipped tweezers to grasp the tick as close to the skin's
  surface as possible.
- Pull upward with steady, even pressure — don't twist or jerk, which
  can cause the mouthparts to break off and remain in the skin.
- Clean the bite area and your hands with soap and water or an
  antiseptic after removal.
- Do not use heat, petroleum jelly, or nail polish to try to make the
  tick detach — these methods aren't recommended and can increase
  disease transmission risk by causing the tick to release more saliva.

## General self-care measures

- Note the date of the bite and keep the tick if possible (in a sealed
  bag or container), which can help with identification if symptoms
  develop.
- Monitor the bite site and overall health for several weeks after the
  bite.
- Take precautions to prevent future tick bites in tick-prone areas
  (protective clothing, repellent, checking skin after being outdoors).

## When to seek medical attention

- A rash (especially an expanding one), fever, or flu-like symptoms
  developing after a tick bite — see [[lyme-disease-care]].
- Inability to fully remove the tick, or mouthparts remaining in the
  skin.
- A tick bite in an area known for tick-borne diseases, even without
  symptoms — discuss with a doctor about monitoring or preventive
  treatment.
- Signs of infection at the bite site (increasing redness, warmth,
  pus).

## Common pitfalls

- **Using folk remedies (heat, petroleum jelly) to remove a tick** —
  these methods are not recommended and can increase infection risk
  compared to prompt mechanical removal.
- **Twisting or jerking the tick out** — this increases the chance of
  the mouthparts breaking off and remaining embedded in the skin.
- **Not monitoring for delayed symptoms** — some tick-borne illness
  symptoms (like the Lyme disease rash) can appear days to weeks after
  the bite, not necessarily right away.
