# Crucial Conversations

A "crucial conversation" is one where stakes are high, opinions differ,
and emotions run strong — exactly the conditions under which people tend
to communicate worst (silence or aggression) rather than best. This skill
summarizes the practical toolkit from the book *Crucial Conversations:
Tools for Talking When Stakes Are High*.

## 1. Get unstuck with CPR

Before addressing a recurring conflict, name which of three things is
actually the issue: **Content** (the single current instance of a
problem), **Pattern** (a recurring behavior over time), or
**Relationship** (how the problem is affecting the relationship itself).
Addressing a Pattern-level or Relationship-level issue as if it were only
a one-off Content issue is a common reason the same conflict keeps
recurring.

## 2. Start with heart

Before speaking, check: what do I actually want here — for myself, for
the other person, for the relationship, for the organization — and would
my next move actually get me that? People under stress often start
behaving in ways that get them the opposite of what they say they want
(winning the argument instead of solving the problem).

## 3. Master my stories

Emotions come from the story we tell ourselves about what happened, not
directly from the facts. Separate the two by asking three questions that
counter common self-serving narratives:

- **Victim story** — "What am I pretending not to notice about my own
  role in this?"
- **Villain story** — "Why would a reasonable, rational, decent person do
  this?"
- **Helpless story** — "What should I do right now to move toward what I
  really want?"

## 4. STATE my path

Share a difficult message persuasively, not abrasively: **S**hare the
facts, **T**ell your story (clearly labeled as your interpretation, not
fact), **A**sk for the other person's path, **T**alk tentatively (leave
room for being wrong), **E**ncourage testing (invite pushback rather than
demanding agreement).

## 5. Learn to look

Watch for the moment a conversation *becomes* crucial (stakes rising,
emotions engaging) and for **silence** (understating, avoiding, staying
quiet) or **violence** (overstating, labeling, attacking) — in yourself
and in the other person — as the two failure modes safety breakdown
produces.

## 6. Make it safe

When safety breaks down, restore it before continuing the actual content
of the conversation:

- If there's a clear problem, rebuild mutual respect (e.g. a genuine
  apology) and restate or create mutual purpose.
- If it's actually a misunderstanding, use **Contrast**: state what you
  don't mean, then what you do mean, to correct a misread intention
  quickly.

## 7. Create mutual purpose via CRIB

When two people's purposes genuinely conflict, work through CRIB:
**C**ommit to seek mutual purpose, **R**ecognize the purpose behind each
side's strategy (the stated position vs. the underlying need), **I**nvent
a new mutual purpose (often a higher-level goal both sides actually
share), **B**rainstorm new strategies that could satisfy it.

## 8. Explore others' paths via AMPP

To draw out someone who's gone silent or become defensive: **A**sk to get
them talking, **M**irror their tone/emotion back to confirm you've
noticed it, **P**araphrase what you've heard to show understanding,
**P**rime — offer a guess at what they might be feeling/thinking when
they're not offering anything, to give them something to react to.

## 9. Move to action via WWWF

Close a crucial conversation with explicit follow-through, not a vague
sense of agreement: **W**ho is doing what, **W**hat exactly are they
doing, **W**hen will it be done by, and how/when will we **F**ollow up.
Skipping this step is why a conversation can feel resolved in the room
and then quietly fail to produce any actual change.

## Common pitfalls

- **Treating "start with heart" as a one-time check** rather than
  returning to it whenever the conversation starts to go sideways — it's
  meant to be a recurring self-check, not a single pre-conversation step.
- **Sharing your story as if it were fact** instead of explicitly labeling
  it as your interpretation (the "T" in STATE) — this is often exactly
  what triggers the other person's defensiveness.
- **Skipping straight to problem-solving before safety is restored** —
  CRIB and content-level negotiation don't work while the other person
  feels unsafe; Make It Safe has to come first when silence or violence
  appears.
- **Ending without WWWF** — an emotionally resolved conversation with no
  explicit who/what/when/follow-up plan is a common reason the same issue
  resurfaces.
