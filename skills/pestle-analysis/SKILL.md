# PESTLE Analysis

PESTLE (also PESTEL) analysis is an extension of the original **PEST**
framework — a macro-environmental scanning tool used in strategic
planning to assess external factors a business doesn't control but must
plan around.

## What each letter covers

- **Political** — government intervention in economies: trade policy,
  tax policy, political stability, regulation.
- **Economic** — market conditions: growth rate, exchange rates,
  inflation, interest rates, unemployment.
- **Social** — cultural trends and demographic factors: population
  changes, lifestyle shifts, education levels, attitudes.
- **Technological** — R&D activity, automation, rate of technological
  change, innovation incentives.
- **Legal** *(the "LE" PESTLE adds to PEST)* — laws and regulation
  specifically (as distinct from the broader "Political" category):
  employment law, consumer protection, industry-specific regulation.
- **Environmental** *(the second addition)* — ecological and environmental
  factors: climate, sustainability expectations, environmental
  regulation.

## History

Francis Aguilar developed the original PEST framework in 1967 as an
"environmental scanning" tool to help managers understand a business's
external conditions for strategic planning purposes — it has also
historically been called ETPS analysis (same four factors, different
letter order).

## How it's used

A PESTLE analysis gives an overview of macro-environmental factors
relevant to a business — indicating market growth or decline, competitive
position, and the potential direction for future operations. It's
typically done as an input to strategic planning, alongside (not instead
of) an internal-facing framework like [[swot-analysis]] — PESTLE
covers the external environment broadly; SWOT's "Opportunities" and
"Threats" quadrants are often populated directly from a PESTLE analysis's
findings.

## Variants

- **PESTEL** — the same six factors as PESTLE, different ordering of the
  acronym; functionally identical.
- **STEEPLE** — PESTLE plus **Ethical** considerations.
- **STEEP** — a reordering/condensing of the core elements.
- **PMESII-PT** — a military-planning variant (Political, Military,
  Economic, Social, Information, Infrastructure, Physical environment,
  Time).
- **TELOS** — a project-management-focused variant (Technical, Economic,
  Legal, Operational, Scheduling), assessing project feasibility rather
  than business strategy broadly.

## Limitations

- The framework works better **retrospectively than predictively** — the
  macro-environment is highly fluid, and named factors can shift in ways
  that weren't foreseeable when the analysis was done.
- A common criticism: **listing factors without analyzing their
  cause-and-effect relationship** to actual business outcomes — an
  exhaustive PESTLE table that never gets connected to a specific
  strategic decision is activity, not analysis.
- Like SWOT, PESTLE is a **static snapshot** — it needs periodic
  refreshing to stay useful, not a one-time exercise filed away.

## Common pitfalls

- **Treating PESTLE as a checklist to complete** rather than a lens for
  generating specific, decision-relevant insights — the value is in what
  a factor implies for a specific strategic choice, not in populating
  every category exhaustively.
- **Confusing "Political" and "Legal"** — Political covers government
  policy direction and stability broadly; Legal covers the specific,
  current regulatory/legal requirements a business must comply with. The
  distinction is exactly why PESTLE split "Legal" out from PEST's
  original "Political" category.
- **Doing PESTLE once and never revisiting it** — given the framework's
  own acknowledged weakness at prediction, an analysis more than a year
  or two old should be treated with real skepticism, not assumed still
  valid.
- **Skipping the connection to SWOT/strategy** — a PESTLE analysis that
  doesn't feed into an actual strategic decision (or a
  [[swot-analysis]] Opportunities/Threats assessment) is analysis
  without an application.
