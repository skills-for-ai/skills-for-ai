# Cognitive Task Analysis (CTA)

Cognitive Task Analysis (CTA) is a research methodology for
understanding the cognitive processes and mental strategies individuals
use while performing a task — the knowledge, decision-making, problem-
solving, and other cognitive activities involved, bridging the gap
between observable behavior and the underlying thinking that produces
it. See [[task-analysis]] for the more general technique this specializes.

## Key aspects

- **Interviews** — interview experienced individuals who perform the
  task, using open-ended questions to learn about thought processes,
  decision-making strategies, and problem-solving techniques.
- **Think-aloud protocol** — ask a participant to verbalize their
  thoughts and actions while performing the task in real time —
  observations, decisions, and reasoning spoken out loud as they happen,
  rather than reconstructed afterward.
- **Cognitive Work Analysis (CWA)** — examine the interplay between an
  individual's cognitive processes, the social and organizational
  context, and the task itself — mental models, task strategies, and
  knowledge structures.
- **Knowledge elicitation techniques** — extract explicit and tacit
  knowledge via methods like knowledge maps, concept mapping, and
  decision trees, capturing cognitive structures and the relationships
  between pieces of task-relevant knowledge.
- **Cognitive task interviews** — present participants with specific
  task scenarios and probe for their thought processes, problem-solving
  approaches, and decision-making strategies in that scenario.
- **Cognitive workload assessment** — measure the cognitive workload
  experienced while performing a task, via subjective ratings,
  physiological measures, or performance measures.

## Why the cognitive layer matters

A purely observational task analysis can document *what* someone does
step by step, but misses *why* — the expertise, judgment calls, and
mental shortcuts that make an expert faster or more accurate than a
novice performing the identically-described steps. CTA specifically
targets that gap, which matters most for tasks with real expertise or
judgment involved (diagnosis, troubleshooting, high-stakes decisions)
rather than simple, mechanical procedures.

## Common pitfalls

- **Relying only on retrospective interviews** — asking someone to
  recall their reasoning after the fact is prone to rationalization and
  memory gaps; pairing interviews with think-aloud protocol during actual
  task performance captures more accurate, in-the-moment reasoning.
- **Treating experts as uniformly articulate about their own expertise**
  — much expert knowledge is tacit and hard to verbalize; knowledge-
  elicitation techniques (concept mapping, decision trees) exist
  specifically because direct questioning often isn't enough.
- **Ignoring cognitive workload** — documenting decision steps without
  understanding how mentally demanding they are misses a key factor in
  where errors or slowdowns are likely to occur.
- **Applying CTA to a simple, low-judgment task** — the depth CTA
  provides is worth the effort for expertise-heavy tasks; a simple,
  mechanical procedure is usually better served by a plain
  [[task-analysis]] or [[hierarchical-task-analysis]].
