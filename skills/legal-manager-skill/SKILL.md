# Legal Manager Skill

**This describes the in-house legal function as an organizational role —
it is not legal advice.** Law is jurisdiction-specific and fact-specific;
a real contract, dispute, or compliance question needs a qualified lawyer
licensed in the relevant jurisdiction, not a general description of what
the function typically covers.

## What the function typically owns

- **Contract review and drafting** — reviewing/negotiating agreements
  (customer contracts, vendor agreements, employment agreements,
  partnerships) to ensure terms match business intent and acceptable risk,
  and maintaining templates/playbooks so common terms don't need
  re-negotiating from scratch every time.
- **Corporate compliance** — ensuring the organization meets applicable
  regulatory obligations (varies enormously by industry and jurisdiction:
  data protection, employment law, industry-specific licensing/
  regulation, anti-bribery/corruption, sanctions).
- **Intellectual property** — protecting trademarks, patents, copyrights,
  and trade secrets, and managing IP terms in contracts (who owns
  work product, licensing terms).
- **Risk and liability management** — identifying legal risk in business
  decisions before they're made (a genuine internal advisory function, not
  only after-the-fact contract cleanup), managing insurance/indemnity
  exposure, and handling disputes/litigation (directly or via outside
  counsel).
- **Governance** — supporting the board and leadership on corporate
  governance obligations, and often owning or coordinating the
  organization's policy framework (data protection policy, code of
  conduct, etc.).

## Contract lifecycle, briefly

Draft/receive → negotiate → approve (often via a defined internal
approval matrix based on value/risk) → execute → **manage obligations
during the term** (renewal dates, deliverable/SLA tracking) → renew or
terminate. The "manage obligations during the term" step is commonly the
weakest link in practice — a contract that was carefully negotiated but
then never actively tracked for its renewal date or ongoing obligations
provides much less protection than the negotiation effort implies.

## Working with the rest of the organization

Legal is most effective engaged **early**, as a risk-identification
partner in a decision, not only at the end as a rubber stamp or a blocker
discovered too late to influence the deal terms. A common organizational
antipattern is treating legal review as a final gate on an already-agreed
deal, which structurally forces the function into being an obstacle rather
than an advisor — the fix is process, not lawyer temperament: bring legal
in during structuring, not just before signature.

## Working with outside counsel

Many organizations, especially smaller ones, use in-house counsel to
triage and manage specialist outside counsel (litigation, specific
regulatory domains, jurisdictions the in-house team doesn't cover) rather
than handling every matter internally — the in-house function's job in
that model includes selecting the right outside expertise and managing
cost/scope, not necessarily doing all the legal work itself.

## Common pitfalls

- **Engaging legal only at the final signature stage** — removes any
  opportunity to shape deal terms before they're effectively locked in by
  business-side agreement.
- **No active tracking of contract obligations post-signature** — renewal
  dates, SLA commitments, and termination windows missed because nobody
  owns monitoring them after execution.
- **Treating a jurisdiction-specific compliance answer as universal** —
  data protection, employment, and corporate law vary substantially by
  country/state; assuming a rule from one jurisdiction applies elsewhere
  without checking is a common, serious error.
- **Substituting general knowledge (including this skill) for actual legal
  advice** on a real, specific matter — this file describes what the
  function does at an organizational level; it is not a basis for a real
  legal decision.
