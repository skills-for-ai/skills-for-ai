# Flywheel Effects

The flywheel effect describes how small, continuous efforts can lead to
a compounding effect over time, resulting in significant progress and
momentum — often used to explain the success of companies achieving
sustained growth and competitive advantage.

Every action taken can have a cumulative effect on overall performance;
as a business continues taking actions that contribute to its success,
momentum builds, creating a positive feedback loop that reinforces and
amplifies its efforts.

## Key stages

1. **Start with small efforts** — take small, consistent actions
   contributing to goals: building customer relationships, improving
   product quality, optimizing processes.
2. **Increase momentum** — momentum is the force that propels the
   flywheel forward, building as consistent actions accumulate.
3. **Achieve breakthrough** — once enough momentum builds, efforts
   begin paying off significantly: a jump in revenue or market share,
   or reaching a critical mass of customers.
4. **Sustain success** — continue consistent actions to keep the
   flywheel spinning and maintain momentum, rather than assuming
   breakthrough is a permanent, self-sustaining state.

## Example

Amazon's flywheel is commonly cited: a customer-centric approach, low
prices, and fast shipping reinforce each other in a feedback cycle —
each improvement makes the others more effective, compounding into
market dominance over time.

## Common pitfalls

- **Expecting fast results** — the flywheel model explicitly describes
  a slow-building compounding effect; expecting an early breakthrough
  misunderstands the mechanism.
- **Interrupting consistent effort once momentum builds** — the
  "sustain success" stage matters as much as the earlier ones; stopping
  the actions that built momentum lets the flywheel slow down.
- **Confusing a flywheel with a single silver-bullet initiative** — the
  model depends on multiple reinforcing elements working together, not
  one big lever pulled once.
