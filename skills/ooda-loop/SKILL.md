# OODA Loop

The OODA loop — Observe, Orient, Decide, Act — is a decision-cycle theory
developed by military strategist **John Boyd**. Its core claim: the side
that cycles through observation, orientation, decision, and action faster
than its opponent — including the time to transition between cycles — can
"get inside" the opponent's decision cycle and gain a decisive advantage,
independent of raw power or numbers.

## The four stages

- **Observe** — gather information about the environment, the adversary
  (or competitor), the decision-maker, and the context.
- **Orient** — the pivotal stage: evaluate the information, make logical
  connections, and get inside the mind of the adversary so a response can
  be shaped against their actual capabilities, not a generic playbook.
  Boyd treated Orient as far richer than a simple filter — it's where
  mental models, culture, and prior experience shape what the same raw
  observation even *means*.
- **Decide** — choose a course of action, balancing the need for speed
  against the need for care.
- **Act** — execute quickly, then immediately return to Observe to assess
  the results (including the adversary's reaction) — the loop, not a
  single pass, is the point.

## Why it's a loop of loops, not one loop

Boyd emphasized that "the loop" is actually a set of *interacting* loops
running continuously, not a single linear sequence — and that the ideal
allocation of energy across those loops shifts depending on the phase of
the conflict. The common "rapid OODA loop" pop-summary understates how
much deeper and more comprehensive Boyd's actual theory is.

## Applying it to business

The same structure operates in competitive business: decision-makers
observe the market, form hypotheses about customers/competitors/products
(orient), decide, and act — repeating continuously. Aggressive, conscious
use of the cycle favors **agility over raw power**: a smaller competitor
cycling faster can beat a larger, slower-reacting one, even without more
resources.

## OODA vs. DMAIC vs. PDCA

Three cycles with a similar underlying shape, from different origins:

| Purpose | OODA | DMAIC | PDCA |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Identify/measure the problem | Observe | Define, Measure | Plan |
| Analyze the problem | Orient | Analyze | Plan |
| Create/execute action plans | Decide, Act | Improve | Do |
| Verify/analyze results | Observe (again) | Control | Check/Study |
| Create/execute improvement plans | Decide, Act | Control | Act |

OODA (military origin, emphasizes speed and adaptability against an
adversary) sits alongside DMAIC ([[plan-do-check-act]]'s Six Sigma
cousin, data-driven process improvement) and PDCA
([[plan-do-check-act]] itself, general iterative improvement) — all
three formalize the same observe/plan → act → review → adjust shape with
different emphasis and vocabulary.

## EBFAS: organizational climate for maneuver

Boyd used a German acronym, EBFAS, to describe the organizational climate
that makes fast OODA cycling possible:

- **Einheit** (oneness/unity) — a shared outlook that lets subordinates
  act with initiative while still realizing the commander's intent.
- **Behendigkeit** (agility) — the ability to break out of longstanding
  patterns even when they've been successful.
- **Fingerspitzengefühl** (fingertip feeling) — intuitive situational
  awareness and the ability to respond tactfully and appropriately.
- **Auftragstaktik** (mission-type tactics) — emphasizing the outcome of a
  mission over dictating the specific means of achieving it.
- **Schwerpunkt** (focus of main effort) — a heuristic for prioritizing
  decisions from tactics up to strategy, so every level of an
  organization knows what matters most and why.

## Common pitfalls

- **Treating OODA as "just move fast"** — Boyd's actual emphasis was on
  Orient as the deepest, most decisive stage; a fast Observe-Act loop with
  a shallow Orient step reacts quickly to the wrong read of the situation.
- **Running OODA as a strictly linear, one-shot sequence** — the theory is
  explicitly about interacting, continuously-running loops, not a single
  pass through four boxes.
- **Centralizing decision-making while claiming to use OODA for agility**
  — Boyd's organizational-climate ideas (Auftragstaktik, decentralized
  command) are part of what makes fast cycling possible; a rigid,
  top-down decision process undermines the speed the loop is meant to
  provide.
