# SWOT Analysis Skill

SWOT analysis is a strategic planning framework organized around one core
distinction: **internal factors** the organization controls (Strengths,
Weaknesses) versus **external factors** it doesn't (Opportunities,
Threats).

## The four quadrants

- **Strengths** — internal characteristics giving the organization a
  competitive advantage (human resources, physical assets, finances,
  reputation).
- **Weaknesses** — internal characteristics placing the organization at a
  disadvantage relative to others.
- **Opportunities** — external elements the organization could exploit to
  its advantage (market trends, economic conditions, legislative changes,
  demographic shifts).
- **Threats** — external elements that could cause harm.

The internal/external split is the framework's defining structural
choice — Strengths and Weaknesses are usually considered internal;
Opportunities and Threats are usually considered external. This is
exactly where [[pestle-analysis-skill]] connects to SWOT: PESTLE's
macro-environmental scan is a common, structured way to populate the
external Opportunities/Threats quadrants rather than guessing at them.

## History

The concept originated in 1965 through two parallel efforts: the Stanford
Research Institute's Long Range Planning Service developed a related
"SOFT" framework (satisfactory, opportunities, faults, threats), while
Harvard Business School faculty published *Business Policy: Text and
Cases*, establishing the internal/external division that SWOT still uses.
The "SWOT" acronym itself emerged by 1972 and became widely adopted after
that.

## Typical process

1. Identify internal and external factors across all four quadrants,
   usually via team discussion or workshop.
2. Evaluate the relative importance of each identified factor — a long
   unranked list under each quadrant is less useful than a short,
   prioritized one.
3. Examine relationships **between** quadrants, not just within them — a
   Strength that could address a specific Threat, or a Weakness that
   would block capturing a specific Opportunity, is where SWOT's real
   strategic value shows up.
4. Present results, commonly as a 2×2 matrix, and connect the findings to
   an actual strategic decision.

## Common criticisms

- **Findings often go unused** — a well-documented, real criticism is
  that organizations frequently produce a SWOT matrix and then don't use
  its output in the later stages of actual strategy formulation; the
  exercise becomes a static artifact rather than an input to a decision.
- **Static analysis in a dynamic environment** — like
  [[pestle-analysis-skill]], SWOT captures a snapshot and doesn't
  naturally account for how competitive conditions shift.
- **Personal bias in factor selection** — what counts as a "Strength" or
  "Weakness" is often shaped by whoever is in the room, not by objective
  measurement.
- **Over-weighting external factors** can produce a reactive strategy
  (responding to Threats/Opportunities) rather than a proactive one
  built from genuine Strengths.

## Common pitfalls

- **Populating quadrants without prioritizing** — an exhaustive but
  unranked list in each box is harder to act on than a short list of the
  few factors that actually matter most for the decision at hand.
- **Confusing "internal" and "external" placement** — a factor phrased as
  a Weakness ("we lack market share") that's actually a symptom of an
  external Threat ("a well-funded competitor entered our market") gets
  the analysis backwards and can misdirect the response.
- **Stopping at the matrix** — per the well-documented criticism above,
  the highest-value step (relating quadrants to each other and to an
  actual decision) is exactly the step most often skipped.
- **Never revisiting the analysis** — treating a SWOT done once as
  permanently valid, when the framework's own known limitation is that
  it's a snapshot in a fluid environment.
